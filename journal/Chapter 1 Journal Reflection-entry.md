Chapter 1: the Pathology of everyday things notes

I agree with the beginning of the chapter that many everyday things are difficult to use due to poor design and I guess technically user ignorance. This chapter brings up how important it is for a designer to take these things into consideration, making them also have to perform the roles of both engineer and teacher as well. I found the section on page 3 about the doors to be both funny and teaching. The author, Don Norman reminisced on how his friend “got trapped” in the doorway of a post office, which did not have any labels or clear signifiers for exiting. Having this key element in your design is essential, because otherwise the average non-designeer/engineering, non-critically thinking person may not be able to solve their way through whatever you produced. Essentially, everything needs an instruction manual, but the design part comes in where you can effectively communicate it without overwhelming the user, who is probably already anxious and confused. Choices are scary, and hands need to be held, and that’s okay because it creates and maintains a need for more designers,engineers, teachers, and psychologists, which is funny because isn’t that the name of the chapter? Not really? Oh.

Further into the chapter, Norman reminds us of how overwhelming and stimulating life can be, considering the “amazing number of everyday things, perhaps twenty thousand of them” (page 11). 
The designer’s job is to make those millions of things easily consumable and solvable for a user, otherwise it’s a poor design. Everything about this sounds taxing, for everyone involved. 
I found the section on page 16 about the designer having to put themselves in the user’s mindset interesting. This makes the following parts on page 19 funny, I suppose. Norman explains how easy it should be for a user to use a phone with just a few buttons on it, however then goes into detail about the multiple diligently crafted features hidden within the invention. The diagram on page 20 shows several graphics of the features, unless already being familiar with the physical features of the new device. The designer has to be an interpreter for the engineer and work as a bridge between them and the user. This designer may not even understand how all the features work, but then it’s up to the user to fill in the gaps during translation.
The rest of the chapter bashes on the mapping of a basic phone, which I thought was pretty funny.
